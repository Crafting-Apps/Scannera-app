The basics

What is an OCR scanner?

When you photograph a page, your phone stores a picture of some words. It has no idea what they say. To the software, the sentence is just a pattern of light and dark, no different from a photograph of a tree.

OCR, short for optical character recognition, is the step that closes that gap. It looks at the shapes on the page, works out which letters they are, and hands back actual text: characters you can select, copy, paste and search rather than pixels you can only look at.

An OCR scanner is simply a scanner with that step built in. You point it at a printed page, and instead of only getting an image back, you get the words too.

On device

OCR that works offline

This is the part that makes Scannera's text recognition different from most of what you will find.

A great many OCR apps are a thin shell around a web service: your page is uploaded, recognised on someone else's servers, and the text is sent back. That works, but it means every document you read has to travel, and nothing happens at all when the signal drops.

Scannera ships the recognition model inside the app. The work happens on your phone's own processor, which has two consequences worth stating plainly:

  • It works with no connection. You can recognise text in airplane mode, in a basement, or on a train going through a tunnel.
  • The page does not go anywhere. There is no upload step in recognition, because there is nothing to upload to.

The trade-off is that the model has to be small enough to ship with an app, which is what shapes the limitations further down this page. The offline document scanner page covers which other parts of Scannera work the same way.

The workflow

Extract text from scanned documents

  1. Scan or import the page. Capture it with the camera, or bring in a PDF or image already on your device. Edge detection and perspective correction straighten it first, which matters more for recognition than people expect.
  2. Run recognition. The bundled model reads the page on your phone. No upload, no waiting on a server.
  3. Review the result. Read what came back against the page. OCR is a best effort, not a guarantee, and this is the step people skip.
  4. Copy or share. Take the recognised text out as plain text, into an email, a note, a message, or anywhere else you need the words rather than the picture.

The scan itself is unaffected by any of this. You still have the PDF of the page, filtered and annotated as usual; the recognised text is something you get in addition to it, not instead of it.

Privacy

OCR without uploading your documents

The documents worth running OCR on are rarely trivial: invoices with account numbers, letters from a doctor, contracts, forms with your address on them. Recognition happening on the device means those pages are read where they already are, rather than being sent somewhere to be read.

It would be overstating it to say nothing ever leaves your phone, so here is the honest version. Scannera uses the network in three places, none of which is text recognition: verifying a Pro purchase through Google Play, syncing Google Drive backup when you have turned it on, and sending anonymous crash reports and usage analytics. That diagnostic data contains no document content.

Google Drive backup is optional and off by default, and when enabled it copies files to your own Google account. The private document scanner page goes through the architecture, and the privacy policy states each network use in full.

Being straight with you

OCR limitations

Text recognition is genuinely useful and genuinely imperfect. Knowing where the edges are saves you a bad surprise.

Which scripts the bundled model covers

Recognition uses a model shipped inside the app. It covers English and the other languages written with the Latin alphabet, along with Hindi and Marathi in Devanagari, Chinese, Japanese and Korean. It is not a universal, every-language engine, and Scannera does not claim to recognise scripts the bundled model was not built for.

Results depend on the scan

A flat, well-lit, in-focus page of printed text reads far better than a creased receipt photographed in dim light at an angle. Most disappointing OCR results are really disappointing capture results, so it is worth taking the shot again rather than fighting the output.

No accuracy figure is claimed

You will see scanning apps advertise a recognition accuracy percentage. Scannera does not, because the honest answer depends entirely on the page in front of you. Treat the output as a fast, useful draft and proofread anything that matters: a figure on an invoice, a reference number, a name.

Search matches names, not recognised text

This one catches people out, so it is worth being explicit: the in-app search looks at document and folder names. It does not search the text recognised inside your scans. If you want to find a document later by something written on it, put that in the document name when you save it.
